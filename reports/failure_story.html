Stage 2 · one change — normalize

Plot each bladder against what we paid for.

Raw cycles can't be compared — a 290-cycle part and a 1,680-cycle part live on different planets. So we put every part on one axis: the diagonal is its target. On or above the line, it did its job. Below the line, it broke a promise.

No statistics needed. of scrapped bladders sit below the line.

How to read it: x = target cycles, y = actual cycles, both log. The 45° line is where actual = target. Distance below the line = fraction of the promise unmet.

Stage 3b · one change — cause

If it were random, every column would be a mix.

They aren't. Columns come out near one pure color — one part leaks, another deforms, almost exclusively. A near-pure column is the eye's version of a significant result, without saying "p-value" to the room.

Leak is a made-part problem; deform is a same-delivery-different-outcome problem — ours, on the floor. The vocabulary carries into every later stage.

Stage 4 · one change — time

A bad lot clusters by arrival, not by death.

Your question was lot defects — so we key time to install date, when a part was made and delivered, not when it happened to die. If a delivery were bad, the reds would bunch into a vertical band.

They don't — the reds are spread across every quarter. That points away from "a bad shipment" and toward something in the plant.

caveat — survivorshipThis sheet holds only bladders already scrapped. Long-lived units still running aren't here, so the recent months look short. Read the right edge with that in mind — it's the first thing a sharp judge attacks, so we say it first.
