Stage 1 · experience

Thirty years of instinct — zero evidence.

The people who run these machines already know which bladders misbehave, and they have a hunch why. But a hunch doesn’t win a meeting with the design team, and it doesn’t win a claim with the maker.

So we did one thing: we wrote down every bladder that died, and let it answer the two questions they couldn’t.

Stage 2 · the record

On paper, it’s just a list.

A target life, an actual life, a cause, a machine. Nothing jumps out — the evidence is in here, but the table hides it. We have to change how we look.

The fair-fight filter
Stage 3 · a fair fight

We judge only the bladders that got a real run.

The ones that blew on the first tire often never make it into the record — so counting them would be guessing. To stay honest, we keep only bladders that reached at least half of their promised life. No cherry-picking the disasters.

— that’s the evidence base everything below is built on.

Why 50%: instant infant-mortality isn’t reliably logged. Restricting to ≥50% of target means every part is judged on a fair run, so a manufacturer can’t wave the result away.

Stage 4 · pinpoint

Two colors. Two completely different problems.

Plot each bladder against its own promise (the diagonal). Below the line, it broke that promise — and the color says who owns it:

Blue = leak. A sealing failure baked into the part — that’s the manufacturer. Amber = deform. Same delivery, wildly different shape — that’s how we run it.

If it were one problem, the reds would be one color. They aren’t. That split is the whole case — and now we prove each half.

How to read it: x = target, y = actual life (log). 45° line = kept the promise. Color = recorded failure cause.

Stage 5 · proof it’s us

Same part. Only the machine changed.

It’s the identical bladder — same maker, same size, same delivery. The one thing that differs is the press it ran on. A part can’t be defective on one machine and excellent on another. This is us — and it’s fixable in weeks, not a design cycle.

Stage 6 · proof it’s them

This one is short — the same way, every time.

That tightness is the tell. When we cause a failure, outcomes scatter — different setters, different presses, different lives. This doesn’t scatter. It lands in the same short spot every time, always a leak — the fingerprint of a defect built into the part. That’s the make and size we hand to the maker: fix it, or give us a benchmark unit.

The board: what to push up first — and what it buys us.

Each variant, where it sits today against its promise (100% = benchmark), how far a known fix can lift it, who owns the fix, and how soon it pays.

Read it as a sequence. The us rows are ours to move now — standardise the setter and fix the worst presses; the same part already proves 2–4× on a good machine, so the ceiling is real, and the payback is one to two months. The them row is the maker’s: the 8″ turnup dies short on every press — hand them the make and size as a redesign target or a benchmark claim.
